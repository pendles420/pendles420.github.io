San Francisco and Manhattan’s best techno-financial workers tripped on their basic, oppressive reality. They knew they had to, because anything else was too disgusting and confusing. I am growing into a beautiful man. “I am this awareness, the awareness of a human experience,” the rich young man thought while despondently holding his laptop in one hand and his phone in the other, all while wearing sandals. If one derelict area was like this, the whole thing must have been. Citizens generated false magic in the commons. The citizens were allowed small dogs. Eyebrows thinned into imperceptible tracings and bushed out again to the user’s despair. Everyone got their way. Online, people debated about whether a wheelchair user ran over a homeless person’s palm on purpose or not. Controversy stirred while Tupperware morphed back into glass and holistic ideals returned for people afraid of (but who secretly worshipped) the fact of artifice. The poor lived in excess and the rich were more desperate than ever. The concerned, tempurpedic mother community spewed rabid, fantastical explanations of life into the sky, up there. The Mother Community. Factions splintered and tweezers grew paltry. “My children all have minor scoliosis. The children drive our mini-vans at night. My child ain’t rebel enough,” one mother reported. My sweet trash smell entered the nostril as a gigantic light-up text display suggested reforestation as a solution to extending our five final years, but before I could nervously laugh the absurdity off, a worker’s ladder bonked my head and I saw Barbara Streisand young. Children end up having creepy journeys into adulthood. They slowly reach towards senescence with clawing-like movements. Upon reaching maturation, they end up behaving in myriad ways that signal a breach, a betrayal of their childlike inanity. Reason, deep-anger, and otherworldly repressive forces all constitute the spiritual template of a man. Our protagonist, Cryss, rejects this paradigm and forcibly attempts a wrathful implantation of typically kiddish styles. “I’m wearing pink pants like a kid,” he cries out while whipping back and forth on a swing, eventually circling over the top bar of the swingset. “I can fall off and not get hurt. Children have smaller terminal velocities. It’s hard for a child to die without the introduction of immense tragedy (which is usually avoidable anyway),” Cryss says in a calm, high-pitched voice, seemingly unaffected by his gyrating movements and grown up body. “I like to pretend my body is small like a tot!” Cryss calls out finally before jumping off, sticking the landing in sand, foamily and unhurt. Reason and logic end up restricting people into behaviors they don’t enjoy. Even reasonably spawning chaos or splendor through roleplay or mental age regression wasn’t enough for our protagonist, or protagonists, Cryss. That’s right, we as writers can multiply her, alter her gender, shrink her and kill her, only to revive and enlarge her into a foamy, expanded form. “I want to be normal now. I want to grow up and act like the other adults found at institutes,” she begs. Eschewing uncertainty, I immediately facilitate her desire, not intending to teach her some kind of Buddhist lesson about the thing you wish for being the thing that unravels and destroys you. I am not a dogmatic narrator. I am quite leftist, though. A dog with words on it licked slowly in your direction, but it did not constitute as proof that a narrative was unfolding. No. You had to go to therapy to get granted your story, be religious, survive getting cancer. Or. You could try waiting for things to boil and spill over, then the water could drip down into the oven’s wiring and start a big oven problem. But still, nothing was happening. You found yourself “paying attention to the smaller things,” like in that movie that everyone on tumblr liked because it said to romanticize your life. Smelling the roses. Writing poems. Winking at each other, your personal jokes. You are clinically depressed, you are just fine. You laughed and spoke in a high voice with your girlfriend late at night, catalyzing feelings of living beauty in each other, all of your selves and memories bouncing and angling around, prismatically set free in the gaze of love. And yet, nothing. There was no secret. No stone yet overturned revealing anything other than a worm or Mexican gum wrapper. A walk in the park and a simulation of progress, returned home to satisfaction that wears off after a while, must dose up again. A thousand ketamine raves. It did not, effectively, “last.” But that was okay, everything was temporary and nothing “lasted.” You knew you would die, and it would be intense, so you were suggested to take grave note of this grave fact, in order to greaterly appreciate your life and be more intentional inside of it before you died. Death makes life more meaningful. You told yourself Buddhist things, absorbed philosophies, focused on work, calling your friends, starting families. My little ones, I am so proud of my little ones. Your little ones ran around and looked like you. As a professor you analyzed culture and cross-referenced texts to explain the predicament of the late-somethingism. As a homesteader, you slowed down and sanded wood, you intimately sanded wood, while smiling at your wife peering in the shed door. The green hills glowed and the stars drooled over your hut. You lived like this with a small hole of nothing located somewhere in your basketball-shaped head until one day you found something in your backyard. A book. Of course, it’s always a book. The secrets to the universe. But this book wasn’t about the universe, and it wasn’t a metaphor for something beyond the meaninglessness/meaningfulness binary. It was an actual book with content in it that would change your life. Sucking up every word like a hummingbird sucking hummingbird water out of flowers, your pupils dilated and you thought things like, “I wrote this. This is my book. Oh my god.” The book was like an instruction manual or a high fantasy novel. It had no conflict. It was written between three hundred and three hundred thousand years ago. It mentioned smartphones. It didn’t know what it was. One line said: “In the history of the universe, the word “world” cried the most.” “Oh my fuckin’ god!” You shouted, drooling down onto the page. Your wife came running from inside and you punched her instantly. Tonight was your night and you would go out on the town and try to score some H and then go home and pray and sleep for a perfect eight hours, wake up and intentionally crash your car on the way to work, but you’d show up anyway and do a surprisingly good job, even impressing your coworkers with your intellectual wit and earnest candor. You would smile creepily from here on out.
